4. Trip-to-Master Pipeline

Done

  • Export / import a trip SHIPPED — move a trip between machines as a unit, as a small ZIP. Came up while deciding what should happen when the master is not reachable: the answer there is "that is an error", which only holds if there is a proper way to carry work from one machine to the other. What travels is what is hard to recreate (the trip's identity) or easy to forget (its species list, lifer baseline, hand-verified translations) — the pictures themselves move separately, by an ordinary file copy. Bringing work back the other way uses exactly the same export and import, so there is no separate "return" behaviour to trust.
  • A species is learned once, and then known everywhere SHIPPED — naming a species that was not yet in your list means a lookup on the internet. That lookup used to be thrown away by the species editor, saved to whichever list happened to be in front of the culling viewer, and only reached the master when you ran Publish Species Names by hand — so the same species was looked up again in the next trip and again on the other machine. A newly recognised species is now saved the moment you commit it, to the shared master list and the trip's own copy, and the next step in the pipeline already knows it. It is written by appending rather than rewriting the whole 2 MB list per species, which also spares Dropbox. If the master cannot be reached you are told, and nothing is lost — reconnect and it saves. Edit Species List is now a single entry (it was one per trip and one per master, and which one you opened decided who saw the correction). This also retires the planned GBIF lookup disk-cache — there is nothing left to re-look-up. Carrying a change made on the laptop into the master still needs a merge: Publish Species Names does that, and is now a reconciliation step rather than the only way the master ever learned anything.
  • The new-species count now stays true, whatever you merge next SHIPPED — Count Species tells you which species on a trip were new to you. It used to answer that by asking whether the species was in the master right now, which only worked as long as you read the report before merging the trip: afterwards its own species were in the master and every new species silently stopped being new. Nothing recorded what the answer had been. A trip's new species are now judged against everything the master has a picture of from before the trip's start date, worked out afresh on every run. It cannot be thrown off by merging this trip, nor by merging an older trip afterwards — a species first photographed in March counts as known on a May trip even if the March trip only reaches the master in July, which is exactly the case that produced a wrong count (48 instead of 47 on Greece) before this landed. The report names the rule it used, says how much of the trip it covers, and a day that was never summarized is named rather than quietly contributing nothing.
  • Landscapes are no longer mistaken for species SHIPPED — landscape/topic entries are stored looking exactly like species (2026, filed under topics/landscape/) and reach the counting code through the day summaries. They were only being kept out of the report by accident, and sat in the species lists behind it. They are now discarded as the summaries are read, and the report states how many it ignored.
  • The trip's species verdict now outlives the trip SHIPPED — Merge Pictures into Master starts by counting species and storing the result in the master: the full report, the species list it was judged against, and a row in admin/trip_species_summary.csv (species seen, new species, dates). It runs first because that is the last moment the master is still free of the trip, and it goes into the master because the trip itself does not survive — retiring a trip deletes its whole repository, and resetting one clears the day summaries the report is built from. "How many new species did that trip give me?" is now a question you can still answer years later, for every trip, without opening a single text file. Retire Trip warns when a trip is about to be deleted with no archived count, since that number cannot be recovered afterwards.

6. Taxonomy & Species IN PROGRESS

Restarted 15 October 2026, because the 2 TB master makes every later reorganisation more expensive. Design and findings: docs/plans/taxonomy_order_research.md and docs/plans/taxonomy_order_design.md.

  • IN PROGRESS Species order — the “040 passeriformes” number leaves the name and becomes a sort number among siblings. Decided: GBIF gives names and placement for every group; the order comes from AviList for birds (what Observation.org follows), from a hand table for kingdoms, classes and the orders of insects, mammals and plants, and from the author's own book order for dragonflies and butterflies; the rest is alphabetical. A GBIF merge is never followed on its own — too many are wrong (the European Large Skipper filed under the Asian one, recent damselflies lumped into a relative); the author decides per species after review.   • PARKED Placeholder-species cleanup — the “? <name>” placeholders, coordinated cleanup.
  • PARKED Regional most-likely-birds (eBird).
  • PARKED Aliases for common names — the collection settled on its own names (IOC 10.5 with GBIF), and every list from outside disagrees somewhere. Comparing the two Brazil lists put a number on it: 86 of the 576 birds they share are named differently, and merging all four lists in the trip found 98. Three different problems wear the same face:
    • Spelling convention — Gray/Grey, Golden-Plover against Golden Plover. Mechanical, and the widest slice of the 98.
    • A genuinely different name for the same bird — Bank Swallow against Sand Martin, Mealy Amazon against Mealy Parrot. No rule derives one from the other; it has to be recorded.
    • A split or lump, where an old name does not mean one species any more. This is the dangerous one: an alias that guesses here silently files a photo under the wrong bird.
    The shape: a table of alias → scientific name, per language, with where it came from, living beside the other species metadata and learned as we go — when a list's name does not resolve but its scientific name does, that pairing is an alias worth offering to keep. Two rules hold it together: an alias points at a scientific name and never at another common name, so no chains form; and an alias that would be ambiguous after a split is refused rather than guessed — better to leave a name unresolved than to file it under the wrong species.
    Where it would pay: reading a tour operator's list, keywords typed in the field coming back through the pipeline, the waarneming.nl import, and searching for a species by a name you happen to remember. Until then the checklist and keyword steps do the narrow version of this — group by scientific name and keep our own spelling — which handles the first two kinds but knows nothing about splits.

14 Oct — what the conversion now implies. From 0.4.1 a new master starts from the author's own species list (see ch. 20), so every master made before this chapter is done is a master this chapter has to convert. While only the author's masters exist, the conversion can be a one-off script, run once and checked by hand. Once other people have masters it has to be a migration inside the program: it recognises which list a master is on, takes a backup, converts in place, survives being interrupted, and is safe to run twice. It touches three layers: the species list itself (“040 passeriformes” becomes “passeriformes” plus a sort number — the list has eight orders twice today, numbered and unnumbered); everything that refers to those parents (the master's CSVs, trips, albums); and the published website, whose page paths and links carry the numbered names. The scientific names of species do not change — splits, lumps, moves between families and aliases do, and plants are harder than birds. Announced in the README as planned work, so beta users know it is coming — without promising a conversion (14 Oct): “your collection needs to be converted along with it”. A route that needs no migration code: a collection that is not too large can be read back recursively into a trip and committed into a new master. A second language other than Dutch (ch. 24) rewrites the same list and is best designed alongside.

17. Capture time & time zones

Not scheduled in the coming month (agreed 18 August 2026) — what shipped works and is measured; the storage decision below is deliberately left to sit.

A photo's capture time is really two facts: the instant (a point on the world's timeline, which decides where a tracklog says you were standing) and the local reading (the wall clock where you stood, which decides the day folder and what a caption says). A camera records the local reading; the instant is only recoverable if it also recorded its UTC offset. Treating these as one thing caused everything below.

To do

  • Decide how capture time is stored — the one open decision, and a big one, because it touches sidecars, data_files.csv, the website and the master. Today the instant is known but only lives in the sidecar; nothing downstream uses it. Three shapes were considered:
    • A — keep the camera's own reading and offset (what ships today): 10:30:55+01:00. Faithful, identical to the RAW, instant correct. The local reading is the camera's wall clock, which may not be the one you stood under.
    • B — normalise to true local time: 11:30:55+02:00. Same instant, displayed time matches reality. But it writes an inference into every picture, the sidecar then disagrees with the RAW forever (PhotoLab reads both), and with no tracklog for the day we would be guessing the zone again.
    • C — store the instant explicitly: a UTC column alongside the untouched local time. Nothing reinterpreted, both facts first-class, old rows simply empty. Costs a column and a pass through sv2ReadDirExif.
    Nothing forces this now: a zoned value cannot currently reach the CSVs (sv2 reads the EXIF group, which only comes from RAWs we never write), so the two parsers that would choke on a zone are safe until we deliberately send them one. Related and folded into the same decision: where a day's true zone lives (it may not be recoverable later, though the day-folder name already records its practical effect), and whether to add offline location→timezone lookup, which would settle the Nikon daylight-saving ambiguity for any picture with GPS. Full reasoning and the archive survey: docs/plans/timezone_plan.md.
  • Live-test the 🛰️ row action on the Days panel — built the same way as Unfiled's and never clicked. Small: open a Day, double-click the 🛰️ cell, confirm the review dialog opens on that day with its own log pre-loaded. The equivalent on Trip and Unfiled has been used live; Days is the last of the three.
  • Thinning the tracklog's points NOT WORTH IT NOW (measured 18 August 2026) — the old note proposed thinning the cleaned track to about one point every 5 minutes before correlation, on the grounds that altitude barely moves faster than that. Measured on the largest real log (4,341 points over 8.2 h, one every 6.8 s): parsing costs 10.6 ms, and the altitude cleaning 285 ms — 65 µs per point, and it scales linearly (verified to 69,456 points), so there is no hidden blow-up. The whole current log collection is four files and under a second in total. Nothing to win.

    There is also a flaw in the original idea: the same point list serves position lookup, not just altitude. Thinning before correlation would trade the sub-metre placement we now get for a saving nobody can feel. If a multi-day GPX ever makes a Read visibly slow, the right shape is to thin only the input to the altitude cleaning — that is where all the cost is — and keep full density for position. The trigger to revisit: roughly 70,000 points costs 4.4 s, so a multi-day phone log at 1 Hz (a few hundred thousand points) is where it starts to show.

    Worth noting the accuracy side cannot be settled from this trip's data: the pictures were taken from near-static positions (a whole day spans 150 m), so thinning changes almost nothing and the numbers wobble without trend. That is evidence it would not hurt this kind of shooting, not evidence that it is safe in general.
  • No bulk rewrite of existing timestamps DECIDED — all 75,050 master rows carry bare local time and not one carries a zone. Those values are correct as local readings. Recovering instants for the archive is a re-read of RAWs that can be done later, for whatever subset actually needs it.

  • Tracklog geotagging now uses the camera's own clock offset SHIPPED (17 August 2026) — pictures geotagged from a tracklog could land kilometres from where they were taken, and nothing looked wrong, because both the right and the wrong coordinates were plausible places on the same island. Two independent causes. First, the OM-1 log's timestamps were read as local time when NMEA times are always UTC, which moved the whole track two hours; the log's own date is now used as well, so a track running past midnight UTC no longer stamps its later fixes a day early. Second — and the real problem — the camera's clock offset was searched for by correlating photo times against the track. 18. Away from home — trip readiness

Everything in this chapter only bites when the nearest spare drive, second computer or willing helper is a few thousand kilometres away. None of it is a missing feature in the ordinary sense — the pipeline works — and that is exactly why these items never came up: at home each one has an easy way out. Raised and scoped 18 August 2026.

Two risks were considered and dismissed on the facts, so they are not on the list. Losing pictures: there are plenty of SD cards and no expectation of filling them, so the cards stay as the original copy for the whole trip — the RAWs are never the thing at risk. Working offline: each operation already gates on its own real dependency (publishing on SFTP, species lookup on GBIF, place names on Nominatim), so a hotel with no wifi degrades rather than breaks. What is genuinely exposed is the work: the culling decisions, species identifications, locations and blog text that exist only on the laptop.

Where this premise runs out — see chapter 22. “The cards keep the pictures” holds only while there is a spare card in the bag. Emptying a finished card, once every frame on it is provably accounted for, is how the premise is bought back rather than abandoned.

How this chapter gets built — keep it simple. In ten years no memory card was ever lost; what was lost were CurrentPictures directories, when SpeciesViewer went rogue and deleted files. That is the honest risk record, and it points the opposite way from clever design: a sophisticated feature carries failure modes nobody saw coming, and the more carefully specified an item on this list becomes, the more expensive and the more dangerous it gets. So each item below is to be built as the smallest independent function that nothing else depends on — something the daily flow neither calls nor notices, that can be run, ignored or deleted without consequence. Where an item as written here is more elaborate than that, the write-up is the ambition, not the build order: cut it down first. Too good means we get nothing.

To do

  • Nothing watches free space, and a failed copy doesn't stop a Read SHIPPED (2026-08-19) — was the highest item here. No part of the program ever asked the operating system how much room was left. In the card copy, a failure on one file logged a warning and added one to a counter; the dialog then reported its ordinary “Copied N, skipped M, failed K” line, in the same tone whether K was zero or eight hundred. A laptop drive that filled up in week three therefore produced a Read that looked like every other Read, with a number in the middle of a sentence as the only sign.

    What was built. A free-space question is now asked before the copying starts, at the one moment the answer can be exact: the card read works in two phases, and by the end of the first one the program knows precisely which files it is going to write and how many bytes that is. If they do not fit — with a gigabyte of working room left over for sidecars, caches and simply not running the machine to zero — the read stops there and says so in plain numbers: needs 42.3 GB, only 11.1 GB free, nothing was copied. Nothing is half-done and the card is untouched. What the first phase already worked out is still saved, so once room has been freed the re-read picks up quickly and skips whatever did get copied.

    The second half is about tone. A read that lost pictures no longer ends on a green Done. It ends on a warning that counts the losses, names the first few files, and says the obvious useful thing — run the Read again, what already arrived will be skipped. This mattered more than it sounds: the closing line is written by whichever step finishes last, and when place-name lookup ran after the copy it used to report its own clean success over the top of a failed copy. All the endings now share one marker, so that cannot happen.

    One judgement worth recording: a single unreadable file does not abort the read. It finishes, copies everything it can, and reports loudly. A bad sector on one frame should not end a two-thousand-picture import. Only the up-front space check refuses outright, and it refuses before anything has been written.

    STILL OPEN the same check wherever else the program writes in bulk: freeze, adding to the master, building previews. Each knows its own byte total, so each is now a single call to the shared check.
  • Say how full every drive is, at start-up SHIPPED (2026-08-19) — the counterpart to the check above, and the cheaper half. Rather than only refusing a copy at the moment it would fail, the program now states the situation once, unprompted, when it starts: one line per volume with its size, its free space and the free percentage. On BigBoy that is five drives plus whatever card happens to be in the reader. Below ten per cent free reads as a warning, below five per cent as an alarm. It answers the question before it is asked, and unlike the pre-flight check it is visible on a day when nothing is going wrong yet — which is exactly when a drive filling up is still cheap to deal with.

    Network drives are deliberately left out: asking a disconnected share how big it is can take seconds, and nothing on the start-up path may wait that long. Empty card-reader slots are skipped for a related reason — asking them at all can raise Windows' own “there is no disk in the drive” box, which is silenced across the sweep. The whole report runs off to one side while the program carries on starting, so a drive that has spun down delays nothing.

    One thing turned up while building it: the log panel had no colour for errors at all. Warnings were tinted, and two specific phrases were painted red, but an error printed in exactly the same ink as a routine line — which is the failure this panel exists to prevent. Errors are now red everywhere in the program, not just here.
  • Reserve room on the card for its own ingest cache SHIPPED (2026-08-19) — SpeciesViewer keeps a small state file on the card itself (.sv_ingest_cache.json, in the folder being read) holding each file's checksum and capture date. It is what makes a second Read of the same card fast, and it travels with the card, which is the point: read a card on the laptop and later on BigBoy and both know what was already imported. It is small — a few hundred kilobytes for a couple of thousand files — but it is saved by writing a temporary copy first and then renaming, so a save briefly needs room for two of them. On a card with nothing left the write fails, and the failure was swallowed deliberately, because the same code must tolerate a write-protected card. Nothing was lost; the card simply re-hashed itself from scratch every time, which is exactly the kind of slow annoyance one does not want in the field.

    Is it worth guarding at all? Arithmetic first, because it decided the answer. Pictures are about 18 MB, so the space left over on a card shot to the end is essentially a random amount between nothing and one picture — under a megabyte roughly one card in eighteen. Per card that is negligible. Across the ten or so cards a long trip fills it is about a coin flip, and it lands on the evening you least want to spend twenty minutes re-hashing.

    What was built. A one-megabyte dummy file lives on the card, and it is deleted only after a save has actually failed: try to save, fail, spend the reserve, try once more, put the reserve back. The ordering is the entire design. The obvious alternative — delete the dummy before every write and restore it afterwards — would have SpeciesViewer deleting a file on the card on every single read, and the card holds the only original copy of the pictures. That is the same class of action that has actually cost data here before. This way the destructive step exists only on the one path where it is what saves you; on every ordinary read nothing on the card is touched.

    The reserve is laid down at the start of a read, which is when the card is most likely to still have room. On a good day a card can go from empty to full, and it typically gets read more than once along the way: the lunchtime read leaves the reserve while the card is half empty, and the evening read spends it.

    The other idea considered was to pad the cache file itself to its maximum expected size so it never has to grow. It does not survive contact with how the file is saved: the temporary copy means two copies exist at once regardless, so padding to maximum needs twice the maximum — worse, not better. It only helps if the atomic save is abandoned as well, and then an interrupted write leaves a corrupt cache. Survivable, since it is only a cache, but it trades a certain small cost for an occasional total one.

    The swallowed failure is now a visible one-line warning, but only when the card proved itself writable by giving the reserve up. A write-protected card stays as quiet as it always was, which is the normal condition the original silence existed for — no guesswork or error-code sniffing was needed to tell the two apart.

    One honest caveat on the testing: every branch was proven, but the full-card branch was proven by making the save fail on purpose, not by filling a real card to within a megabyte. The behaviour is right; the first genuine encounter will still be in the field.

    STILL OPEN the dummy has a name no scan can mistake for a picture, but nothing yet removes it on request — a card handed to somebody else still carries it. And the laptop-side fallback for a card that can never be written (a cache keyed by card fingerprint, the way the destination hash cache already works) remains unbuilt; it loses the travelling property, which is why it is a fallback and not the design.
  • Back up the day's work to a spare card — one ZIP per day SHIPPED (2026-08-20, both halves) — the cards keep the pictures safe, so this is not a picture backup: it is a backup of the work. A fortnight of culling, identification, locations and blog text lives on a single laptop, and re-doing it is a fortnight of evenings nobody gets back. There are two very large spare cards to write it to.

    The measurement that settled the design. A real day — 202 pictures — is 4.1 GB of RAW and 1.6 MB of sidecars. Two and a half thousand to one. At that ratio there is no argument for a thin nightly backup and a fat occasional one: take everything, every time. Adding the rendered JPGs at about 2 MB each still leaves a fortnight fitting several times over on a 16 GB card. The first real run bore it out — a whole trip, 3217 files, came to 309 MB and about two minutes, and the second run finished in seconds because nothing had changed.

    What a day's ZIP holds: the sidecars that carry every culling decision, the PhotoLab recipes, the rendered JPGs and the day's blog text. Never a RAW — those stay on the cards, which is the whole premise. Each trip gets its own folder on the card, so the card lists trips rather than forty anonymous ZIP files.

    How it knows what changed is the quiet cleverness of the thing. Each ZIP carries a small index of what went into it, and the next run compares the folder against that index. It notices deletions — which is the entire point, since culling is mostly deleting — where the obvious "has anything been modified lately?" test would miss them completely. The same index also records every picture filename that existed, and the filename prefix the cards were read with, both of which the restore will need.

    A hole closed on the way. That prefix — the “2026 Goeree” in “2026 Goeree - N8140001.ORF” — turned out to live in exactly one place: a per-machine scratch file that never leaves the laptop. Lose the laptop and it is gone, and re-reading the cards with even a slightly different prefix would orphan every sidecar and look precisely like a mass deletion. It is now recorded with the trip itself, so it travels with the trip export, and the Read dialog offers the right one automatically. A smaller everyday bug went with it: switching trips used to suggest the previous trip's prefix.

    In use it is a column in the trip overview beside Geotag, one cell per day. The symbol reports rather than decorates — backed up, changed since, never, nothing to back up — so the question “what is protected?” is answered at a glance instead of only when asked. Clicking opens a picker with that day ticked and the others one tick away, and whatever has changed is pre-ticked, so the evening job is one click.

    The restore, and the thing that reframed it. The rule from the day this was drafted — the restore direction gets tested before the trip, not after it, because a backup nobody has restored from is a belief rather than a backup — has now been kept. What changed on the way is the story it is built for. The obvious one is the laptop dying in a hotel room, and that story makes the restore a rare emergency tool. But exporting a trip to the laptop and importing it back at home is the normal path, done every trip, and the day ZIPs have to fit into a round trip that already exists. Designed against the homecoming rather than the disaster, the restore is something used often enough to stay trustworthy.

    Three things come back, in order, and only the first is new. Import Trip restores the trip's own identity: its settings, species list, lifer baseline and translations — the gap flagged above, now closed by writing the trip export onto the card alongside the day ZIPs. Restore work from a card unpacks the day ZIPs, putting back every sidecar, PhotoLab recipe, rendered JPG and blog text. Then the RAWs are read back off the SD-cards, which is where they have been all along. The card that carries all this is now labelled SV INDEX, so the one in the bag that matters is identifiable by name rather than by size.

    Re-applying the culling is the part that had to be right. An SD-card holds every frame ever shot; a plain re-read therefore puts back a fortnight of pictures that were deliberately thrown away, and the culling has to be done again from scratch. The backup already records which pictures each day still held, so the restore reads the cards through that list: a real trip day came back as 94 pictures kept out of 352 on the cards, with the other 258 never copied. Days the backup has never seen are copied in full — an unknown day must not be mistaken for an empty one.

    One obstacle was purely physical and worth recording, because no amount of design around the data would have found it: there is one card reader. Inserting the SD-card takes the SV INDEX card out — often at the same drive letter — so at the moment the restore needs to know what survived, the card holding that knowledge is in a pocket. The surviving-picture list is therefore captured onto the laptop the moment the index card is readable, and it lives with the trip rather than with the machine, so it travels. The cards can also be read from the restore screen itself, which keeps the whole sequence on one screen instead of sending you to another dialog halfway through.

    The day list on the read screen says what the numbers mean: beside the count of frames on the card there is now what was deleted during the trip and what is kept. 202 shot on a day against 21 wanted is the truth of culling, and reading only the 202 invited the thought that something had gone missing.

    ONE CAVEAT sidecars made before 17 August 2026 do not record the camera's UTC offset — that fix came later — so a restore from an older backup returns them exactly as they were, and the automatic tracklog geotag has nothing to work from on those days. Their GPS itself comes back untouched; only the automatic pass abstains, and the manual Geotag from tracklog… still does the job. Worth one more rehearsal, too, on a machine where the pictures really are absent: the trial runs had the RAWs still on the disk, so the culling filter chose correctly but the copy itself had nothing left to do.
  • Putting a deleted picture back SHIPPED (2026-08-19) — the machinery already existed and worked: a delete moves files to a trash folder with a manifest, and a single call puts them back. Nothing in the interface called it, so in practice an accidental delete was permanent whatever the trash still held. Bulk delete by colour is precisely the evening job in a hotel room after a long day, and it was the one action with no way back.

    What was built is a picker rather than the single “undo last” button the line above imagined. Deletes stack — put one back and the next one is waiting — and popping that stack a dialog at a time tells you nothing about what else is underneath. The screen lists every delete the trash can still reverse, newest first, with the date, the picture, how many files and how large; tick any number and they all go back in one pass.

    The thumbnail is the point of the whole screen. A name like 2025 tanzania-nb069275 does not say which bird you threw away, and the pixel size matters more than one would guess: among the 27 pictures sitting in the trash today are several 2000×270 and 539×100 banner strips — web headers, deliberately deleted — which from the name alone look exactly like the photographs above them. RAW files are previewed from the JPEG the camera embedded, and that is labelled as such, because a preview is smaller than the sensor image and quoting its size as the picture's would understate every RAW in the list.

    One design note worth keeping. The list is rebuilt from the trash every time, never patched: a delete appears because its files are still in the trash, and disappears because they are not. Emptying the trash and restoring a picture both remove it by the same test, so the screen cannot drift out of step with the disk, and cannot offer the same picture twice — with no bookkeeping to get out of date.

    The Database panel was tidied at the same time. The trash size line and its two buttons now sit together under a Trashed images heading, because Empty trash is the one action that makes restoring impossible and belongs in the same eyeful. The two Restores were given names that tell them apart — Restore backup rolls the master files back to a snapshot, Restore images puts deleted pictures back. The section covering the write-back queue was renamed too: called Pending writes, it read as though the database were having trouble writing, when the truth is the opposite — the edit is safely in the database and what is outstanding is copying it back into the master CSVs. And the Save button was removed: it covered exactly one field on a panel where every other control acts immediately, so it implied unsaved state that was never there. That field now saves when you leave it.
  • The log is erased at every start, and lands wherever the program was launched from SHIPPED (2026-08-19) — studio.log was opened in “write” mode under a plain relative name, so every start threw away the previous session and the file's location depended on how the program happened to be started. Something odd happens on Tuesday, the program is restarted, and by Wednesday there is nothing left to look at — which matters far more when there is no second machine to reproduce it on.

    It now lives at a fixed address beside the other per-machine application data, and the last five sessions are kept alongside the current one. The rotation is deliberately by session rather than by size: away from home the log worth reading is usually the one from the run before the one being stared at, and a size-based scheme would happily fill all five slots from a single chatty afternoon. If a second copy of the program is holding the file, the rotation is skipped and the new run appends rather than refusing to start.

    The log also announces its own location on every start, which is the small piece that makes the rest useful: the file moved out of the launch directory, so that line is now the answer to “where is the log?” — and the first thing wanted when asking somebody to send one.
  • Rehearse the whole trip once, on the laptop, before leaving NEEDS TEST — several paths a trip depends on are built but have never been used in anger. Create Trip and Select or Update Trip were split into two one-directional dialogs after Create Trip overwrote a live trip's identity, and both have been verified to open but not driven end to end. Trip and Album overview parity has not been exercised live. The virtual-trip preview path is import-verified only. And the trip export / import round trip — the thing that eventually carries the trip home — has never been run in both directions. One evening covers it: create a trip, read a card into it, cull a day, write a blog, publish it, export it, import it back. Cheaper than any single feature on this list, and it is how the remaining surprises get found.
  • Show how old the SmallMaster snapshot is SHIPPED (2026-08-20) — on the road, whether a species is a lifer and which names get suggested both depend on the master snapshot carried on the laptop. Nothing showed when that snapshot was taken, so a stale one gave quietly wrong verdicts with no visible symptom: the answer simply differed from the one the full master would have given, and looked exactly as confident.

    The date now travels with the copy. The export writes it into the ZIP itself, at the root, so unpacking the ZIP into the master directory files it correctly — there is no separate step that can be skipped, and no per-machine settings file to lose. It is written last, from the counts that actually went in, for the same reason the day-backup manifest is: a stamp made up front can describe a ZIP that never finished.

    Four places show it, which sounds like three too many until you notice they answer different questions. The install dialog shows both dates before anything is unpacked — this ZIP against what is already installed — and says so in amber when the ZIP is the older of the two, which is the mistake worth catching. The status bar carries the age beside the trip name, amber past thirty days and red past ninety, because "which data am I looking at" is the same kind of fact as which trip is open. The start-up log records it so that a week later the question what did it know at the time? is still answerable. And the Master Species Viewer's read-only banner carries it too: that screen is where the age actually costs something, since it is the history a lifer verdict is judged against.

    A copy made before any of this existed is not left blank. The ZIP falls back to its own file date, an installed copy to the date on its metadata files, and both say plainly that they are estimates — a guess presented as a fact is worse than no date at all. That paid off within the hour: the first real install reported the copy already on the laptop as 20 days old — 31 Jul 2026 (estimated from the date on the metadata files), which was correct.

    The transfer, which is what the exercise really turned up. Making a fresh snapshot to test with took forty minutes over wifi, and while it ran the program could only say “12,000 files added to ZIP…” — a number that cannot tell you whether that is nearly done or barely started. So the export now counts before it copies: one walk collects the list, the run opens with “26,352 biota file(s), 20.7 GB to write”, and progress reads “13,000 of 26,352 files (49%), 10.2 GB of 20.7 GB, 19m10s gone, ~19m43s left at 9 MB/s”. The estimate is made from bytes rather than files, because the tree mixes 4 MB photographs with 4 KB sidecars and a file-count percentage would promise a finish time that drifts as the mix changes. Progress ticks every 500 files or five seconds, whichever comes first: the old rule was fine on a local disk and minutes of silence on a slow one, which is exactly where reassurance is wanted. Times read as 40m10s, not 2410.9s.

    Writing to a share also now says so before the hour is spent rather than after, since a dropped connection leaves a half-written ZIP that still looks like a file. Only a certain answer warns; a false warning about the drive you always use is worse than none. The numbers make the case on their own: the same job took 1m28s to an SSD against forty minutes over wifi, and the install on the laptop took 39 seconds.

    Then a question worth asking of any backup: what is actually in it? Of 73,061 sidecars in the master, 62,741 belonged to pictures with no JPG — pictures the laptop cannot display at all. Each was traced: the ratings, camera data and dimensions on those cards come from metadata/active, not from the sidecar; the (no image) card is drawn from a CSV row and looks identical either way; the crop mark reads the DxO recipe, which is not in the ZIP, and otherwise falls back to a size comparison from the CSV; and the machine is locked read-only, so nothing ever writes one back. They were 70% of the file count and 3% of the bytes. Leaving them out takes the ZIP from 89,093 files to 26,352 — barely smaller, but the file count is what the install pays for.

    One detail earned its keep. Deciding which sidecar belongs to a picture uses the master's own rule rather than a plain name match, so that dsc_0620-small.jpg and dsc_0620_DxO.jpg both count as the picture for dsc_0620.xmp. A plain match would have silently dropped 2,521 sidecars that do belong to a picture you can see. A configuration key restores the old everything-in behaviour for anyone who wants it.

    One thing deliberately not built: a tool to delete the sidecars already sitting on the laptop from previous installs. It would recover 0.74 GB, and it would do so by walking the master tree deleting files — the one class of action with a bad record here. If the laptop is ever to be cleaned, deleting biota/ and re-installing is simpler, safer and easier to check.
  • “Did this card arrive complete?” OPEN lower priority — every copied file is already checksummed at the destination and a mismatch already raises, so the program knows whether a card arrived intact; it just never says so. One closing line — all N files from this card are verified present — turns that into something a person can rely on. Lower priority than it first looked: with the cards kept as the original copy for the whole trip, nothing irreversible hangs on the answer.

Checklist before departure

Not work items — the things to do in the week before, once the above have been decided: refresh the SmallMaster snapshot and confirm its date shows; rehearse the round trip described above; confirm the backup card is formatted and that its restore direction has actually been tried; check free space against the trip's expected volume; and confirm the camera's clock and time zone match where you are going — the Read dialog now warns, but knowing beforehand is better.

Third-party code and the licence

This is less open than it looks. PyQt6 is offered under GPL v3 or a commercial licence, so any distributed SpeciesViewer must be GPL-3.0. The repository's LICENSE is already GPL-3.0, which means the decision is made and correct. It also means the instinct towards a “non-profit use only” condition cannot be acted on: such a clause would make SpeciesViewer non-free and would breach PyQt's own terms. GPL-3.0 already achieves the thing actually wanted — anybody may use and modify it, but nobody may take it closed.

Code licences and data licences are different questions. There is no Creative Commons code anywhere in the stack. CC governs content, and SpeciesViewer does consume content: OpenStreetMap tiles and Nominatim place names (ODbL, with an attribution requirement and a usage policy), GBIF occurrence and taxonomy data (CC-BY family, per-dataset), and the photographs themselves, which remain the photographer's. The map view already shows OSM attribution; the published species pages and the README need the GBIF citation, and the two obligations should be stated in one place. Corrected 13 Oct: SpeciesViewer uses GBIF for names and classification only and does not publish GBIF occurrence records, so no citation is owed today; NOTICE.md says so, and that a page showing occurrences would have to cite GBIF.

21. Modern Nikon, Canon and Sony bodies

SpeciesViewer was built around Olympus files and grew Nikon support alongside them. Nothing in it is deliberately single-brand, but everything in it has only ever been proven on two brands, and the places where a camera's identity leaks into the code are known. Supporting current Nikon, Canon and Sony bodies properly is a prerequisite for the users chapter 20 hopes to attract: a photographer whose files do not open will not stay to read the architecture document.

The four places a RAW file is touched

Adding a brand is not one job. A file passes through four independent mechanisms, and a body can fail at any one of them while working at the other three:

  • Capture date at ingest — read through exiftool, which supports essentially every body promptly. Lowest risk.
  • Sidecar creation — sv0ReadSDCard._write_sidecars does a blanket -xmp:all<all copy plus one hand-mapped field, -XMP-exif:SubjectDistance<Olympus:FocusDistance#. That single line is the clearest statement of the problem: every maker keeps focus distance under its own tag. RESOLVED 24 Aug for the path that matters — the master scan now merges all four shapes (see below). The sidecar expression above is still Olympus-only, which costs nothing while the website and the panels read the master fields. The parked question in ch.1 about a curated field list is the same question seen from the other side.
  • Embedded preview extraction — the fast path that makes culling usable. Nikon embeds a full-size JPEG; Canon and Sony embed previews of varying size and, on some bodies, only a small one. Where the embedded preview is too small, the viewer has to fall back to a full decode and the twelve-fold speed advantage disappears. Worth measuring per body rather than assuming.
  • Full-resolution decode — rawpy/LibRaw, for the 100% view and for JPG creation. This is where genuinely new formats fail, and where the fix is a library version rather than SpeciesViewer code.

The formats that are actually hard

NEW Three known trouble spots, all in the decode path:

  • Canon CR3 — a different container from CR2; needs a recent LibRaw. Canon also produces HEIF (.HIF) on some bodies. The sidecar half is now settled (ch.7: a HEIC/HEIF original stores its rating in a plain .xmp); what remains is that .hif is absent from the extension table entirely, so such a file is never even seen.
  • Nikon High Efficiency NEF (the HE / HE* modes on Z bodies) — compressed with a scheme that older LibRaw builds cannot read at all.
  • Sony lossless-compressed ARW — several generations of compression, added to LibRaw at different times.

MEASURED 23 Aug Established with real files, against SpeciesViewer's own .venv — exiftool 12.81, rawpy 0.27.0, LibRaw 0.22.1. Of 30 samples covering every body and mode below, 29 decode and one kind of file does not:

  • Canon CR3 is fine. EOS R5, R5 Mark II and R7, RAW and CRAW, full-frame and crop — all decode, in 0.6–1.1 s. No newer LibRaw required.
  • Sony ARW is fine. A1, A1 II (lossless and lossy), A9 III and A7R V all decode. The several generations of compression are simply not a problem at 0.22.1.
  • Nikon HE / HE* does not decode — and no upgrade will fix it. All six High Efficiency files fail (Z 8, Z 9, Z 50 II) while every Lossless file from the same body succeeds. HE is intoPIX TicoRAW, which LibRaw cannot read without a licensed module. So “pin a newer rawpy”, the answer this chapter expected, is the wrong answer here. There is no version to pin.

That matters much less than it sounds. Every HE / HE* file still carries a full-size 8256×5504 JPEG of about 6 MB in JpgFromRaw, which preview_extract pulls out in 0.03 s — so culling, rating, thumbnails, the large viewer and Create JPG all behave normally on a Z 8 or Z 9. Only our own full decode is missing, and what that needs is an honest message rather than code.

ExifTool calls every HE NEF an NRW. A Z 9 file named .NEF reports FileType: NRW and FileTypeExtension: nrw, because HE uses the NRW-style container. Sitting directly on top of the .nrw incident below, that is a trap worth naming: classify by the on-disk extension, never by ExifTool's file type. NEFCompression is the reliable discriminator — 3 is Lossless, 13 is High Efficiency, 14 is High Efficiency*.

Two things the corpus found that were not on the list — both fixed

BUG A Canon R5 Mark II can embed an HEVC preview instead of a JPEG, and we then render a blank card. Two of the four R5 II samples — the two shot in Canon's HDR-PQ variant (CompressorVersion: CanonCR3_003, ColorSpace: n/a) — contain no JPEG at all; their PreviewImage and ThumbnailImage are both H.265 bitstreams. extract_preview_bytes() hands 29 KB of HEVC straight to Qt without checking it, Qt cannot decode it, and the card comes up empty with nothing in the log saying why. The raw data itself decodes perfectly, and the other two R5 II files are ordinary JPEG-preview CR3s — so an R5 II owner would see some pictures appear and some not, depending on how each was shot. The fix is small: check for FF D8 FF before accepting an embed, fall through to the next tag, and fall back to a LibRaw decode rather than to nothing.

SHIPPED 24 Aug on branch SpeciesViewer03, and the bug was worse than written above. extract_preview_to() did not merely fail: its “downscale failed, cache it full size” branch — written for a preview of the wrong size — swallowed one of the wrong format, wrote the 29 KB of HEVC into the preview cache under a .jpg name, and returned success. Since a cache entry that exists is never re-extracted, the blank card was permanent, not just first-render. Three independent pieces now: extract_preview_bytes() magic-checks every embed and is guaranteed to return a JPEG or nothing; decode_preview_jpeg() renders one from a half_size LibRaw decode, deliberately outside the exiftool lock so a multi-second decode cannot stall every other thumbnail in the grid; and preview_jpeg_bytes() combines the two for callers whose job is simply “show this picture”.

Verified across all 30 corpus files: the two broken R5 II files now render at 3200×2136 in about a second, cached like any other preview, and the other 28 are byte-identical to before. Existing poisoned cache entries are 29 KB, so the Remove low-res button in Manage Caches already clears them — no cache versioning was needed.

Two judgement calls worth recording. The Editor's Camera JPEG tab deliberately keeps the strict function and keeps saying “This RAW carries no embedded JPEG”: quietly substituting our own render under a label that claims the camera's would be the exact lie that tab exists to prevent. And Create JPG decodes at full size rather than preview size — that file becomes the picture's usable image and can end up on the website, so a 3200 px render where every other body gets a full-size one would have been a silent downgrade in the one file that matters most.

The log wording changed too, and the lesson generalises beyond this bug. The first attempt said skipped non-JPEG ThumbnailImage (29112 bytes, starts 00000014) — true, and useless to a person. SHIPPED 24 Aug on branch SpeciesViewer03, and the general fix turned out to be smaller than the specific one deserved. Rather than adding JpgFromRaw2 to the list — which would have fixed Panasonic and left the mechanism broken — embed_lengths() now asks exiftool -preview:all and reads the byte count out of its own “(Binary data N bytes)” line. The two fixes needed each other. -preview:all reports embeds the old list never named — the Z 9 also offers PreviewTIFF and ThumbnailTIFF, which are not JPEGs. A week ago, discovering those would have handed Qt a TIFF and produced the blank card this chapter opened with. And it is the same lesson as the extension table, one layer down. PARKED NEW 24 Sep Some Canon bodies write stills as HEIF, with the extension .HIF. SpeciesViewer does not merely handle such a file badly — it never sees it. .hif appears nowhere in the tree: not in the canonical extension table, not in a single line of code. Not pursued in 2026 (decided 2026-10-01).

Why it is worth having is not the obvious reason. No camera in the library writes one, and there is not a single .HIF to be found — nought in the master alongside its 403 .HEIC files, and nought in the 45-file test corpus. The case is a fellow birder’s file: pictures handed over at the end of a shared day, from a body nobody in this library owns. The hard half is already done. A .HIF is a HEIF, the same container as a .HEIC, and chapter 7 settled what that means: such a file is self-describing, holding its own rating, colour, species and caption inside itself rather than delegating them to a sidecar. The write channel, the cache rules and the grouping all exist and are live-proven on 461 real files. So this is a table entry and a proof, not a new mechanism: add .hif beside .heic and .heif in utils/raw_extensions.py — and only there, per the invariant that no extension is ever hard-coded at a call site — then verify the three things that can still differ per format: that exiftool reads the capture date, that a preview can be extracted, and that a rating and a colour round-trip back out of the file.

What makes this a decision rather than a task. Widening the extension table widens what ingest accepts: a folder or card that produced nothing before would start producing pictures, which is a change in behaviour for every existing lane, not only for the one new file. And it cannot be verified without a sample — there is nothing to test against in the corpus or the master, so the first step is asking the birder who has one for a single file, exactly as the two hand-added Nikon Z 8 frames were sourced for the corpus above.

22. Emptying a card, safely

Chapter 18 settled that the cards keep the pictures: there are plenty of them, there is no expectation of filling them, and so the RAWs are never the thing at risk. That premise is not reopened here — it is bought back. It holds only while there is a spare card in the bag, and a long trip eventually runs out. This chapter is how a finished card becomes a blank one again without guessing about a single frame.

Not a backup. Nothing is copied that has not already been judged. The mirror-everything backup dismissed in chapter 18 stays dismissed; what is built here moves only the frames you decided to keep, and only after proving that every other frame on the card was thrown away on purpose.

What was built

  • The gate: every frame on the card must have a verdict SHIPPED (2026-08-25) — the feature is the check; the copying is the easy part. Before a card may be emptied, each picture it holds must be either kept or deliberately thrown away. Anything else stops it dead.

    The roster is the card's own record, never the laptop's day folder. This is the whole safety argument in one sentence. A frame that was shot and never copied is absent from the day folder, so a check against the folder passes it by not existing — and that frame is precisely the one this feature exists to protect. Every card SV has read carries .sv_ingest_cache.json, listing every file it ever showed us, and the per-file last_copied stamp separates ingested and then discarded from never ingested at all. A card SV has never read has no roster, and a card with no roster is unknown, never clear: absence of evidence blocks.

    Seven verdicts, of which three refuse: never ingested, not yet culled, and a name that matched a file of a different size. The rest are outcomes — kept, marked for deletion but not yet deleted, deleted (found in the trash), and deleted (gone from the laptop entirely). Culling moves a condemned stem to .trash with its .xmp rather than unlinking it, so an executed sentence is evidence rather than inference.
  • Two cards, and the source card is the witness SHIPPED (2026-08-25) — the card being emptied is in a slot for its roster and identity, not as a copy source. The pictures come off the laptop, because that is where the prefixed names and the sidecars carrying the culling work live. Two readers, then — unlike the work backup, which was shaped by having only one.
  • The keeper card holds the complete state of those frames SHIPPED (2026-08-25) — RAW, .xmp, .dop and the rendered JPGs, in day folders under one folder per source card. It is self-sufficient: dropped into a trip directory it is ready for Read & Summarize, with no ZIP and no survivor list involved. That is structurally simpler than restoring from the SV INDEX card, which needs three artifacts in the right order; the two restore paths are different animals and are deliberately kept apart.

    There is no setting for what to copy, and that is the design. Measured on a real card: sidecars 0.13% of the RAW payload, .dop 0.06%, renders 0.42% — 0.60% for everything. A choice that can only ever move the total by half a percent is not a choice, it is a question asked on every run forever, and one you would have to answer correctly in advance about a card you rely on months later. The payoff is lopsided too: leaving the renders behind saves twelve megabytes and can cost hours of DeepPRIME. This is the same arithmetic that settled thin-versus-fat in chapter 18 at 2500:1. The one legitimate size question is will it fit, which is a constraint rather than a preference, and is asked as a free-space pre-flight at the moment the byte total is exact.
  • The keepers are complete, and they are old SHIPPED (2026-08-25) — the observation that shaped the rest. RAW is immutable; species names, colours and renders stay fluent for days after a card is consolidated, while the SV INDEX card is refreshed daily and a keeper card is not. So a keeper card states its own staleness in SV_KEEPERS.json (metadata as of), and a re-run refreshes the sidecars and renders while never re-copying a RAW — topping up a keeper card is a twelve-megabyte evening job. The usual rule that a sidecar at the destination is never overwritten exists because that destination may hold work done there; on a keeper card nothing edits anything, and the laptop is unambiguously the source.

    The restore order in chapter 18 already handles the overlap: the ZIP restore only overwrites a differing file with consent, and the card read never overwrites an existing sidecar, so work backup first, keeper card second lands the fresh metadata and silently skips the stale. Reverse the order and you are asked instead. Neither half should be “simplified”.
  • A card that knows which card it is SHIPPED (2026-08-25) — there was no such thing before. The drive letter is whatever the reader handed out this time and the volume label is often blank, which is the original complaint: every card in the bag looks identical. A UUID is now minted into the card's own record the first time it matters, with the Windows volume serial alongside as corroboration. It is written only where SV is already writing — never by the check, which stays a read-only question.
  • Deleting the pictures, after the copy is verified SHIPPED (2026-08-25) — the original rule was that SV never deletes on the source card, because the card holds the only original. A verified copy is exactly what stops that being true. And in practice cards here are emptied by hand rather than formatted, so a tool that stopped at “safe to format” and left the doing to a human was inviting the wrong card to be wiped — the very problem the chapter opens with.

    It has teeth. A separate click, never bundled with the copy. Keepers must be found on the keeper card at the right size or they survive and are reported. Frames discarded during culling have no copy by design, and are counted separately so the confirmation can say how many pictures are about to exist nowhere. It refuses outright if the card's identity does not match the plan, because cards get swapped between one step and the next and this is the one irreversible operation. Files not in the roster — a stray .dat, Windows' own System Volume Information — are never touched, and are counted so their survival is visible rather than silent.

    The card keeps its own record, pruned. Deleting by hand takes the .sv files too; doing it here keeps them and removes only the entries for files that actually went. The identity and the read history are what make the next empty safe, and a card left claiming to hold pictures it no longer has would break its clear stamp on the next trip. SpeciesViewer still never formats anything.

Proven, and not yet proven

The whole cycle has been run on real hardware (2026-08-25): a 2 GB card of 112 Olympus frames checked against the trip, saved to a 60 GB keeper card — 112 RAW, 112 .xmp, 112 .dop, 2.0 GB, byte sizes matching the laptop — then emptied. The card came back 0.1% full, its roster pruned to nothing, its identity and read history intact, and the reserve megabyte and Windows' own folder untouched.

The bug that nearly shipped, and what caught it. Card filenames are un-prefixed (N5040114.ORF); the laptop's are prefixed. With no bridge between them nothing matched — and matched nothing presents in the counts as every frame was deliberately deleted, which is the verdict that says the card is ready to empty. The first real card passed the test anyway, because it had been built by copying already-prefixed files back onto a card, so the two names happened to be identical. The fix is a prefix derived from the two name sets; the lesson is the guardrail added beside it, which now refuses when not one frame can be matched, on the grounds that this is far more likely to be the wrong folder than a card whose every picture was thrown away. A test rig that is too tidy proves nothing.

Not covered. There is no laptop-side list of which cards are clear — the answer lives on each card and is read when it is in a slot, which is the point, but it does mean you cannot survey the bag without inserting each one. A card handed to someone else still carries its record, naming trips; that gap is inherited from the reserve megabyte and is unchanged. And the ordering that keeps a fresh work backup ahead of a stale keeper card is true today because of how the two restores happen to be written, not because anything enforces it.

24. A second language other than Dutch

NEW 14 Oct PARKED Not a high priority, but to keep in mind for the ch. 6 conversion. Today every species carries an English and a Dutch name, and the website, the blogs and the name-display profiles (ch. 16) offer English, Dutch and scientific. A French or German photographer would want French or German in place of Dutch. It may be easier than it looks: the list already has one column per language (DutchName, EnglishName), GBIF and the IOC list carry names in many languages, and blog text already goes through a translator. The questions are which column becomes “the second language”, how the website's NL/EN switch and file names (-nl.html) generalise, and where the interface itself is Dutch. Worth designing together with the ch. 6 conversion, because both rewrite the same species list.
